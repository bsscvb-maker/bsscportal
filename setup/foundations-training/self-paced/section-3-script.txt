The Board helps turn our purpose into decisions the club can carry out responsibly. Directors review proposals, budgets, membership matters, and conduct concerns within the authority our rules provide.

They consider what the club can afford, who will do the work, and whether a proposal serves the organization. They make Board decisions together and record them so people can refer to what actually happened.

Our regular Board meeting is the last Tuesday of the month. Holiday scheduling, including December, may require an announced adjustment. Some decisions remain with the membership. Understanding how directors reach the Board helps explain that shared responsibility.

Eligible voting members elect directors for the announced open seats. The directors then choose the core officers through the officer-election process.

That means elected leadership comes through a process. A position carries duties to the whole club, including people who voted differently or see an issue another way. Directors need to prepare, use their judgment, and follow through.

Each director has one Board vote, even if that person holds more than one role. We keep the detailed election procedures in the governing documents. Remember the basic path: members choose directors, and the Board chooses the officers.

Leadership starts with learning the club. Under the ordinary eligibility rule, a person needs one completed year of active full membership before taking office on the Board. Prospect time does not count as that year.

A new director then spends the first-year learning Board responsibilities before becoming eligible for a core officer role. Prior completed Board service counts, and any authorized exception must follow the formal process.

Eligibility gives someone the opportunity to stand for a position. It does not guarantee election. These expectations give members time to understand how the club works before taking responsibility for leading it.

Two-year director terms allow experience to stay on the Board while seats come up for election. With staggered terms, the club can welcome new directors without having to start over with an entirely new group.

The number of open seats can change. Members should use the announced election information rather than assuming the same number of positions opens every year.

A Board seat and an officer assignment are different responsibilities. The governing documents and any approved transition determine their terms and eligibility. For members, the practical point is continuity: elections bring fresh involvement while experienced directors help carry unfinished work forward.

Every director has a working role. Directors review information, ask useful questions, disclose conflicts, and vote based on what serves the club.

The responsibility continues after a vote. If a director accepts an assignment, that work still needs to be completed and reported back. Good oversight includes checking that an approved decision turns into action.

Also remember that one director's opinion is not automatically an official Board decision. The club acts on decisions made through the proper process. With that distinction in mind, we can look at the officers who coordinate specific parts of the work.

The President coordinates the club's overall work. The responsibilities include chairing meetings, keeping officers connected, following through on approved decisions, and speaking for BSSC.

That means looking across the whole organization. A ride, a fundraiser, a membership question, and a community request may involve different people, but they still need consistent direction.

The President uses the authority of the office and the responsibilities the club has delegated. Decisions that require Board or membership approval still go through that process. When you are unsure where a club matter belongs, start with the President, who can direct it to the right person.

The Vice President works closely with the President to keep club business moving. That includes helping with orientation, checking progress, and following up when an assignment needs attention.

The role also provides continuity. If the President is temporarily unavailable, the Vice President carries out the duties provided in our governing documents.

Members should see the President and Vice President working together and giving consistent direction. If a question involves several people, that coordination helps us avoid missed work and conflicting answers. The next officer supports continuity in another important way: keeping an accurate record of what the club has done.

The Secretary keeps the club's official record. That includes meeting minutes, motions, votes, notices, and important membership and governing-document records.

When someone asks what the club approved, we should be able to find an answer in the record. That helps us avoid relying on a rumor or on several people's different memories.

You can help by giving the Secretary accurate information and reporting corrections promptly. Minutes capture the meeting's actions and decisions. Good records also help new officers pick up where previous officers left off. They protect the club's history and make our current work easier to follow.

The Treasurer keeps our financial information organized and accurate. The work includes receipts, deposits, account records, reports, budget preparation, and required financial filings.

Those records show what we have, what we owe, and which money belongs to a particular purpose. A healthy balance can still include funds that are restricted or already committed.

Members support that work by identifying payments clearly, keeping receipts, and turning money over promptly. Get the required approval before spending or promising club funds. Reliable records help the Board make responsible decisions and help us honor the trust members and donors place in us.

The Sergeant at Arms helps keep meetings and activities orderly and safe. That can mean settling a disruption, offering informal guidance, or responding when someone's behavior creates an immediate problem.

Cooperate with directions intended to restore order or protect people. Arguing about authority in the middle of a disruption makes it harder for everyone to resolve.

Immediate safety action and informal counseling are separate from a final disciplinary decision. Formal discipline follows the club's review process. The purpose of this role is to help people enjoy a safe, respectful club environment. That requires cooperation from all of us.

Now that we have covered the officers' responsibilities, here is what cooperation looks like in practice. When the President gives direction within the responsibilities of the office, members are expected to work with that direction.

If something is unclear, ask. If you disagree, use the established process to raise the concern. Publicly competing instructions or arguments can leave volunteers unsure which plan to follow.

Raise an immediate safety concern promptly. Required Board and membership approvals still apply. Clear direction helps a meeting or event run smoothly, and a clear review process gives people a way to address disagreements.

Respect for the office needs to continue beyond any one person's term. It applies to the current President and to each person who later serves in that office.

A strong club gives its next leaders accurate records, useful relationships, and members willing to work together. We should not have to rebuild those habits every time an office changes hands.

You can have your own views and still cooperate respectfully with the person carrying the responsibility. Supporting a good transition helps protect the friendships, events, and community work everyone enjoys. That also means learning how to disagree without dividing the club.

You are allowed to ask questions and disagree. What matters is how you handle the disagreement.

Explain the issue, give the facts you know, and say what outcome you are asking for. Keep the discussion about the problem. Insults, gossip, and attempts to recruit sides make a concern harder to resolve.

Give the responsible people an opportunity to review it. A serious complaint involving the President has its own reporting route, which we will cover later. Honest concerns should have a fair hearing. For an ordinary club question, the best starting point is a direct conversation with the President.

When you have an ordinary club question and are unsure who should handle it, start with the President. You do not have to figure out the entire organization before asking.

The President can involve Board members as needed. Starting in one place helps us avoid several people giving partial answers or members receiving different versions of a decision.

If the President’s answer does not resolve the concern, explain what remains unresolved and ask for Board review. We will cover that process shortly. You should also know where to find the written rules that guide those answers.

If something in this club bothers you, bring it to the President. Going from one brother or sister to another complaining, repeating rumors, or trying to get people on your side does not fix the problem. 

It creates sides and makes the issue harder to resolve. Bring the facts, explain your concern, and state what you are asking leadership to address. 

You can disagree and raise questions respectfully. Members can seek personal support, report misconduct, and raise concerns without retaliation. 

If the concern involves the President, speak with the Vice President. If anyone is in immediate danger, get to safety and call 911.
